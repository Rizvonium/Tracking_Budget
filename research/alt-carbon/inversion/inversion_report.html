Five key points

  1. Negative susceptibility needs a test, not an assumption. In our synthetic test, a remanent body inverts to negative susceptibility (mean −0.014 SI against a true +0.03). But a −25 nT regional-field error with no remanence gave a larger negative extreme (−0.021 SI) and more negative cells (32 % against 21 %). Under-regularisation gives the same effect. The deciding test is this: with a positivity bound, remanent data cannot be fit (φd/N = 69). Without remanence, the data fit at φd/N = 0.24. Magnetisation vector inversion (MVI), amplitude inversion and GTK Q-ratios then confirm the cause.
  2. Over-fitting is easy to detect with hold-out flight lines. As the regularisation weight β falls from 10⁴ to 10⁻¹, the training misfit falls by about 4 orders of magnitude. Over the same range the error on held-out lines increases from 1.9 nT to 5.7 nT. The best β gives φd/N ≈ 0.5–1, which agrees with the discrepancy principle.
  3. Memory is the real limit at regional scale. A dense sensitivity matrix for a 100 km region with 50 m cells is about 2.6 PB. Each published efficiency approach (wavelet compression, data-space solves, octree meshes, on-the-fly GPU kernels) has a cost. The report lists these costs so that Alt Carbon’s preferred approach can be compared fairly.
  4. The paper is not identified. The best public candidate is arXiv:2602.03857, a GPU and data-space gravity inversion with GTK co-authors. Its version 1 uses Indian gravity data, not Hakku magnetics, so it is probably not the attached paper. We need the PDF.
  5. A research gap exists for Task 4. We found no published work that does a fully sampled 3D joint inversion of gravity, magnetics and a third physics with a latent lithology field. This can be the core of a PhD chapter.

A. Method families

A1. Structural coupling (cross-gradient, joint total variation, Gramian, structure-guided)

Core idea. Different properties are assumed to change at the same places, so the coupling term rewards spatial alignment of gradients rather than any particular numerical relationship between the properties. That makes the approach the safe default when the petrophysical link is unknown or non-unique. Moorkamp et al. (2011) found that cross-gradient beat direct parameter coupling when the assumed relationship was violated. Gramian constraints generalise this: they penalise linear dependence between models, or between their gradients and other attributes, and can couple any number of properties.

Key equations. - Cross-gradient (Gallardo & Meju 2003, 2004): 𝐭(mi,mj)=∇mi×∇mj\mathbf{t}(m_i,m_j) = \nabla m_i \times \nabla m_j, with ϕc=∑i<j∥𝐭(mi,mj)∥22\phi_c = \sum_{i<j}\lVert \mathbf{t}(m_i,m_j)\rVert_2^2. - Joint total variation (Haber & Holtzman Gazit 2013): JTV(m1,…,mK)=∫Ω∑k=1K|∇mk|2+ϵdV\mathrm{JTV}(m_1,\dots,m_K) = \int_\Omega \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^K \lvert\nabla m_k\rvert^2 + \epsilon}\;dV. It is convex, which makes it easier to optimise. - Gramian (Zhdanov et al. 2012): ϕc=G(Tm1,…,TmK)=det[⟨Tmi,Tmj⟩]i,j=1K\phi_c = G(T m_1,\dots,T m_K) = \det\big[\langle T m_i, T m_j\rangle\big]_{i,j=1}^K. Here TT is the identity (correlation coupling) or ∇\nabla (structural coupling).

A2. Petrophysical coupling (PGI / Gaussian mixture, fuzzy c-means, explicit relations)

Core idea. Each cell’s property vector is assumed to come from one of a small number of rock units. Each unit has a characteristic (multivariate) distribution of physical properties. The coupling is a mixture prior, either a Gaussian mixture (PGI) or fuzzy clusters (FCM). Its parameters can be learned during the inversion, so the result is a property model plus a quasi-geology (unit membership) model. Explicit relations such as Gardner, Archie or a regression are the special case of one unit with a known function. They are powerful when correct and harmful when wrong (Moorkamp et al. 2011).

Key equations. - PGI (Astic & Oldenburg 2019; Astic, Heagy & Oldenburg 2020). The prior is p(𝐦)=∏x∑k=1Kπk𝒩(𝐦(x)∣𝛍k,𝚺k)p(\mathbf{m}) = \prod_{x}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{m}(x)\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\big). The smallness term is approximately ϕs=12∑x∥𝐖1/2(𝐦(x)−𝛍z*(x))∥𝚺z*(x)−12\phi_s = \tfrac12\sum_x \lVert \mathbf{W}^{1/2}(\mathbf{m}(x)-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z^*(x)})\rVert^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{z^*(x)}}, where z*(x)=argmaxkπk𝒩(⋅)z^*(x)=\arg\max_k \pi_k\mathcal{N}(\cdot). The GMM parameters {π,μ,Σ}\{\pi,\mu,\Sigma\} are refit after each model update (MAP-EM with conjugate priors). - FCM (Sun & Li 2016, 2017): ϕc=∑j=1M∑k=1Kujkq∥𝐦j−𝐯k∥2\phi_c = \sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K} u_{jk}^{q}\,\lVert \mathbf{m}_j - \mathbf{v}_k\rVert^2, subject to ∑kujk=1\sum_k u_{jk}=1 and q>1q>1. - Explicit relation: m2=f(m1)m_2 = f(m_1), imposed either through ϕc=∥m2−f(m1)∥2\phi_c = \lVert m_2 - f(m_1)\rVert^2 or by substitution.

A3. Geology-based parameterisations (level sets, parametric/shape inversion, implicit geomodels as priors)

Core idea. Instead of inverting for 10610^6 free voxel values, invert for the parameters of a geological model. Those can be level-set functions that define unit boundaries, wireframe or parametric surfaces, or the inputs of an implicit geological modeller (interface points, orientations, fault parameters). Physical properties are then assigned per unit, which couples all physics by construction: one geometry feeds every forward model. The parameter space shrinks to 10110^1–10410^4 dimensions, which makes full Bayesian sampling feasible. The cost is a strong modelling assumption: the unit topology and the per-unit property distributions must be right.

Key equations. - Multiple level sets: 𝐦(x)=∑k𝐩kχk(x)\mathbf{m}(x) = \sum_{k} \mathbf{p}_k\,\chi_k(x) with χk\chi_k built from H(φk(x))H(\varphi_k(x)). The level-set functions evolve by ∂tφ=−vn|∇φ|\partial_t\varphi = -v_n\lvert\nabla\varphi\rvert, where vnv_n comes from the misfit gradient. - Implicit geomodel prior: 𝐦=𝒫(𝒢(𝛉))\mathbf{m} = \mathcal{P}\big(\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big) and p(𝛉∣𝐝)∝∏ip(𝐝i∣Fi(𝒫i(𝒢(𝛉))))p(𝛉)p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathbf{d}) \propto \prod_i p\big(\mathbf{d}_i \mid F_i(\mathcal{P}_i(\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})))\big)\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta}). Here 𝒢\mathcal{G} is the implicit (co-kriging or potential-field) interpolator that maps geological inputs to a unit field, and 𝒫i\mathcal{P}_i assigns property ii to each unit, possibly drawn from a per-unit distribution.

A4. Bayesian sampling (MCMC, HMC, rjMCMC, parallel tempering, SVGD)

Core idea. Draw samples from p(𝐦∣𝐝)p(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{d}) instead of computing a single optimum. Random-walk Metropolis–Hastings is exact in the limit but scales badly with dimension. Gradient-based samplers such as HMC (and MALA) use adjoint gradients to take long, high-acceptance moves. Trans-dimensional rjMCMC lets the data choose the model complexity (for example the number of Voronoi cells). Parallel tempering handles multimodality. SVGD moves a set of interacting particles deterministically toward the posterior, and sits between MCMC and variational inference.

Key equations. - Metropolis–Hastings: α=min(1,p(𝐦′∣𝐝)q(𝐦∣𝐦′)p(𝐦∣𝐝)q(𝐦′∣𝐦))\alpha = \min\!\Big(1, \frac{p(\mathbf{m}'\mid\mathbf{d})\,q(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{m}')}{p(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{d})\,q(\mathbf{m}'\mid\mathbf{m})}\Big). The dimension-robust pCN proposal is 𝐦′=1−β2𝐦+β𝛏\mathbf{m}' = \sqrt{1-\beta^2}\,\mathbf{m} + \beta\,\boldsymbol{\xi} with 𝛏∼𝒩(0,𝐂prior)\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{C}_{\text{prior}}). - HMC: H(𝐦,𝐩)=−logp(𝐦∣𝐝)+12𝐩⊤𝐌−1𝐩H(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{p}) = -\log p(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{d}) + \tfrac12\mathbf{p}^\top\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{p}, integrated with leapfrog steps. - rjMCMC: α=min(1,p(k′,𝐦′∣𝐝)q(k∣k′)p(k,𝐦∣𝐝)q(k′∣k)|𝐉|)\alpha = \min\!\Big(1,\frac{p(k',\mathbf{m}'\mid\mathbf{d})\,q(k\mid k')}{p(k,\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{d})\,q(k'\mid k)}\,\lvert\mathbf{J}\rvert\Big). - Parallel tempering: chains target p(𝐝∣𝐦)1/Tip(𝐦)p(\mathbf{d}\mid\mathbf{m})^{1/T_i}p(\mathbf{m}) and swap states with probability min(1,[L(𝐦j)L(𝐦i)]1/Ti−1/Tj)\min\!\big(1,\big[\tfrac{L(\mathbf{m}_j)}{L(\mathbf{m}_i)}\big]^{1/T_i-1/T_j}\big). - SVGD: 𝐦i←𝐦i+ϵ1N∑j=1N[k(𝐦j,𝐦i)∇𝐦jlogp(𝐦j∣𝐝)+∇𝐦jk(𝐦j,𝐦i)]\mathbf{m}_i \leftarrow \mathbf{m}_i + \epsilon\,\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N\big[k(\mathbf{m}_j,\mathbf{m}_i)\nabla_{\mathbf{m}_j}\log p(\mathbf{m}_j\mid\mathbf{d}) + \nabla_{\mathbf{m}_j}k(\mathbf{m}_j,\mathbf{m}_i)\big].

A5. Variational and amortised inference (VI, normalising flows, simulation-based inference)

Core idea. Turn sampling into optimisation: choose qϕ(𝐦)q_\phi(\mathbf{m}) from a flexible family, such as a mean-field Gaussian (ADVI) or a normalising flow, to minimise KL(q‖p)\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p). Amortised or simulation-based inference (SBI) goes further. It trains a conditional density qϕ(𝐦∣𝐝)q_\phi(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{d}) on simulated (𝐦,𝐝)(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{d}) pairs, so a new survey is inverted in milliseconds. The catch is that it is only as good as the training prior, and it fails under distribution shift unless corrected with physics (Siahkoohi et al. 2023).

Key equations. - ELBO: ℒ(ϕ)=𝔼qϕ[logp(𝐝∣𝐦)+logp(𝐦)−logqϕ(𝐦)]\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\big[\log p(\mathbf{d}\mid\mathbf{m}) + \log p(\mathbf{m}) - \log q_\phi(\mathbf{m})\big]. - Flow: 𝐦=fϕ(𝐳)\mathbf{m} = f_\phi(\mathbf{z}) with 𝐳∼𝒩(0,I)\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I) and logqϕ(𝐦)=logq0(𝐳)−log|det∂fϕ/∂𝐳|\log q_\phi(\mathbf{m}) = \log q_0(\mathbf{z}) - \log\lvert\det \partial f_\phi/\partial\mathbf{z}\rvert. - Amortised neural posterior estimation: minϕ𝔼(𝐦,𝐝)∼p(𝐦)p(𝐝∣𝐦)[−logqϕ(𝐦∣𝐝)]\min_\phi\ \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{d})\sim p(\mathbf{m})p(\mathbf{d}\mid\mathbf{m})}\big[-\log q_\phi(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{d})\big].

A6. Learned priors (VAE/GAN latent priors, diffusion priors, deep image prior, supervised DL inversion)

Core idea. Replace hand-made regularisation with a prior learned from realistic geological realisations. With a VAE or GAN, the model is 𝐦=G(𝐳)\mathbf{m}=G(\mathbf{z}) and the inversion or sampling happens in a low-dimensional latent space; Laloy et al. report compression ratios of 200–500. Diffusion models supply a score-based prior that can be combined with a likelihood gradient for posterior sampling. Deep image prior (DIP) needs no training data: it reparameterises 𝐦\mathbf{m} as the output of a randomly initialised CNN and relies on the network’s implicit regularisation. Supervised end-to-end networks map data straight to models, which is fast but offers weak guarantees and little UQ.

Key equations. - Latent prior: p(𝐳∣𝐝)∝p(𝐝∣F(G(𝐳)))𝒩(𝐳;0,I)p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{d}) \propto p\big(\mathbf{d}\mid F(G(\mathbf{z}))\big)\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z};0,I). - Diffusion posterior sampling: ∇𝐦tlogpt(𝐦t∣𝐝)≈sθ(𝐦t,t)+∇𝐦tlogp(𝐝∣𝐦̂0(𝐦t))\nabla_{\mathbf{m}_t}\log p_t(\mathbf{m}_t\mid\mathbf{d}) \approx s_\theta(\mathbf{m}_t,t) + \nabla_{\mathbf{m}_t}\log p\big(\mathbf{d}\mid \hat{\mathbf{m}}_0(\mathbf{m}_t)\big). - DIP: 𝐦=fθ(𝐳0)\mathbf{m}=f_\theta(\mathbf{z}_0) and θ*=argminθ∥F(fθ(𝐳0))−𝐝∥Γ−12\theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \lVert F(f_\theta(\mathbf{z}_0))-\mathbf{d}\rVert^2_{\Gamma^{-1}}.

A7. Ensemble and approximate UQ (EKI, randomise-then-optimise, Laplace, null-space)

Core idea. These methods approximate the posterior with many optimisations, an ensemble of forward runs or local curvature, so they can reuse a deterministic inversion engine. - EKI is derivative-free. It updates an ensemble with sample covariances, and its solution lies in the span of the initial ensemble. - Randomise-then-optimise (RTO) and randomised maximum likelihood (RML) solve perturbed MAP problems in parallel. RTO adds a Metropolis or importance-sampling correction. - The Laplace approximation uses a Gaussian built on a low-rank Hessian at the MAP point. - Null-space shuttles explore models that fit the data equally well without full sampling.

A8. Large-scale computation (compression, octree, matrix-free, GPU, equivalent sources, distributed)

Core idea. The dense potential-field sensitivity 𝐆∈ℝN×M\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M} is the bottleneck. There are several ways to break it: - compress it (wavelets: Li & Oldenburg 2003); - shrink MM (adaptive octree meshes: Davis & Li 2011; Haber & Heldmann 2007 for EM); - never store it (on-the-fly or matrix-free products on GPU); - shrink NN or regularise the data first (equivalent sources: Soler & Uieda 2021); - distribute it (Dask: SimPEG).

Every UQ method in sections A4–A7 inherits this cost per forward or adjoint call, multiplied by the number of samples, particles or ensemble members.

Key equations. - 𝐝=𝐆𝐦\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{m}, with dense memory 8NM8NM bytes. - Wavelet compression: 𝐆̃=𝒯τ(𝐆𝐖⊤)\tilde{\mathbf{G}} = \mathcal{T}_\tau(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{W}^\top) and 𝐆𝐦≈𝐆̃(𝐖𝐦)\mathbf{G}\mathbf{m}\approx\tilde{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{m}), keeping about 11–5%5\% of the coefficients. - Equivalent sources: 𝐝≈𝐀𝐜\mathbf{d}\approx\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c} with sources fitted window by window (gradient boosting) at 𝒪(window)\mathcal{O}(\text{window}) memory.

A9. Remanent magnetisation (cross-cutting; it decides how the magnetic leg can be coupled)

Core idea. Strong remanence breaks the induced-only assumption 𝐌=κ𝐇0\mathbf{M}=\kappa\mathbf{H}_0 of Li & Oldenburg (1996). There are two standard responses: 1. MVI: invert for a 3-component magnetisation vector per cell, in Cartesian or sparse spherical coordinates. 2. Amplitude inversion: invert |𝐁|\lvert\mathbf{B}\rvert, which is only weakly sensitive to magnetisation direction, for an effective susceptibility.

For joint inversion, a scalar that is safe to couple is the magnetisation amplitude ∥𝐌∥\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert. Coupling raw susceptibility to density is biased whenever remanence is present.

Key equations. - MVI: 𝐝m=∑c∈{x,y,z}𝐆c𝐌c\mathbf{d}_m = \sum_{c\in\{x,y,z\}}\mathbf{G}_c\,\mathbf{M}_c, with 𝐌=κeff𝐇̂0+𝐌⊥\mathbf{M}=\kappa_{\text{eff}}\hat{\mathbf{H}}_0 + \mathbf{M}_\perp. - Amplitude: |𝐁|=Bx2+By2+Bz2\lvert\mathbf{B}\rvert = \sqrt{B_x^2+B_y^2+B_z^2}, computed from TMI through equivalent sources.

One-line reasons (ASSESSMENT). 1. Cross-gradient. Only assumes co-located boundaries. No UQ. Pairwise terms grow as (K2)\binom{K}{2} and suffer from gradient degeneracy in smooth regions. MVI amplitude couples cleanly. Most widely used, with code in Tomofast-x, jif3D and SimPEG. 2. JTV. Similar assumptions; convex. A single term handles any KK, so adding a third property is trivial. Less field history than cross-gradient. 3. Gramian. Can impose a correlation that does not exist. Generalises naturally to KK properties. Mostly the Zhdanov/TechnoImaging line and Vatankhah’s large-scale work. 4. PGI. Needs a unit count and petrophysical samples. Gives unit-membership probabilities but not a calibrated posterior. Scales like a Tikhonov inversion. The GMM extends directly to 3-D property vectors. Scalar κ\kappa is biased under remanence unless ∥𝐌∥\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert is used. Mature in SimPEG. 5. FCM. Like PGI but non-probabilistic: memberships are not likelihoods. Fewer public implementations. 6. Explicit relation. Fragile, and fails silently when the relation does not hold (Moorkamp 2011). Remanence breaks density–susceptibility relations. 7. Level sets. Assume a discrete number of units with known properties. A low-dimensional state can be sampled. Topology changes are awkward. 8. Implicit geomodel Bayesian. Assumes the unit topology and stratigraphy. Best-calibrated geological uncertainty. Each forward model is fast but needs geology-to-mesh resampling. One geometry feeds every physics, so the third property is close to free. Research-grade. 9. MCMC + PT (voxel). Gold-standard UQ, but 10510^5–10710^7 forward calls on 10610^6 cells is impractical without reduction. Fine on reduced parameterisations. 10. HMC. Gradients make it far better than random walk, and autotuning helps further, but it is still costly at 10610^6 cells. Each property adds an adjoint. 11. rjMCMC. Weakest assumptions (parsimony emerges from the data). Prohibitive in 3-D voxels. Cross-physics trans-dimensional moves are complex. 12. SVGD. Kernel collapse in high dimensions underestimates variance. 50–500 particles, each a full gradient. 13. Per-survey VI / normalising flow. Flow capacity limits 10610^6-cell posteriors. Mean-field ADVI under-disperses. 14. Amortised SBI. Inherits the training prior and is brittle under distribution shift. Training needs a huge number of 3-D simulations. Little potential-field literature. 15. VAE/GAN prior. Prior misspecification is the main risk. Latent MCMC is cheap. 3-D training sets are scarce in Indian geological settings. 16. Diffusion prior. Approximate likelihood guidance, plus memorisation risk. Expensive 3-D score networks. Very new for geophysics. 17. DIP. Implicit bias only; a multi-channel output head gives joint properties. No UQ unless ensembled. 18. Supervised DL. Strong train/test assumptions, little physics consistency, essentially no UQ; inference is instant. 19. EKI. Gaussian-ish updates and ensemble-subspace collapse. Derivative-free, so any black-box third solver works. Proven for ERT and IP. 20. RTO / RML. Exact only for linear-Gaussian problems, approximate otherwise. Embarrassingly parallel MAP solves that reuse the existing coupled objective, with an MH/IS correction available. Works with MVI. 21. Laplace. Local Gaussian only, poor for multimodal posteriors. A low-rank Hessian is feasible at 10610^6 cells. 22. Null-space shuttle. Explores equivalent models rather than giving probabilities. Cheap and deterministic.

Ranking headline. No single method wins every column. Petrophysical/structural coupling gives the physics link, sampling or ensembles give the UQ, and compression or reduced parameterisations make it affordable. The best designs combine one from each group (Section C).

C. Top-3 shortlist with experiment plans

Assumed context (not confirmed with Alt Carbon): exploration for mafic/ultramafic rocks suitable for CO₂ mineralisation (cf. Heagy et al. 2022, arXiv:2203.13894). The third property is most likely conductivity or chargeability from DC/IP or EM (serpentinisation, magnetite, sulphides), or vpv_p from seismic refraction.

C1. Three-property PGI, made probabilistic with randomise-then-optimise

Why. It extends the gravity–magnetic GMM coupling directly to a 3-vector [ρ,∥𝐌∥,logσorη][\rho, \lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert, \log\sigma\ \text{or}\ \eta] per cell. It reuses SimPEG at 10610^6 cells. Wrapping it in RTO/RML adds calibrated-ish UQ through parallel perturbed MAP solves.

Experiment. 1. Build a synthetic 3-D ultramafic intrusion with 4 units: host basalt, serpentinite, peridotite, and remanent magnetite-rich gabbro. Simulate gravity, TMI and DC/IP on an octree mesh (∼106\sim10^6 cells). SimPEG + discretize. 2. Compare four configurations: (a) separate Tikhonov; (b) two-property PGI (gravity + magnetic); (c) three-property PGI with ∥𝐌∥\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert from MVI; (d) the same with raw κ\kappa, to quantify remanence bias. 3. Run 32–64 RTO/RML solves on a Dask cluster. Compute the posterior mean and std per cell and the unit-membership probability. 4. Metrics: unit classification accuracy (confusion matrix), property RMSE, 90% interval coverage of true values, volume-of-reactive-rock estimate with credible interval, and wall time and memory. 5. Go/no-go: three-property PGI improves classification of the serpentinite unit by at least 15% over two-property PGI, and coverage stays between 0.8 and 0.95.

C2. Implicit geological model as the joint parameterisation, sampled with HMC or parallel-tempered pCN

Why. Every physics is coupled through one geometry, which makes adding a property essentially free. Dimension drops to about 10210^2–10310^3, so full MCMC with calibrated geometric UQ becomes feasible. This matches the Alt Carbon question “how much reactive rock, and where are its boundaries?”

Experiment. 1. Build the geology in GemPy (autodiff) or LoopStructural from a few interface points and orientations, plus per-unit property distributions 𝒩(𝛍k,𝚺k)\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k) for (ρ,∥𝐌∥,σ)(\rho,\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert,\sigma). 2. Run forward models: prism gravity and magnetics (Harmonica or SimPEG) and DC/IP or EM (SimPEG) on a fixed octree. Use anti-aliased unit-to-mesh assignment (the Blockworlds lesson). 3. Sample with (a) parallel-tempered pCN (Obsidian-style) and (b) NUTS/HMC through JAX or PyTorch autodiff. Compare effective sample size per CPU-hour. 4. Metrics: posterior of contact depth and dip, posterior volume of the target unit, R̂\hat{R}, and ESS. Also run a misspecification test in which the true model has an extra unit missing from the parameterisation. 5. Deliverable: probability-of-unit maps and volume distributions, which feed directly into CO₂-storage capacity estimates.

C3. Structurally coupled voxel inversion (JTV or cross-gradient) with ensemble UQ (EKI vs SVGD vs low-rank Laplace)

Why. This is the fallback when petrophysics is poorly known; structure-only coupling is the safest assumption. The experiment benchmarks the three cheapest scalable UQ routes against a small-scale reference posterior.

Experiment. 1. Use the same synthetic as C1, but give the conductivity–density relation no consistent pattern, to stress-test petrophysical methods. 2. Joint objective with JTV across (ρ,∥𝐌∥,logσ)(\rho, \lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert, \log\sigma), solved with SimPEG, using on-the-fly or compressed sensitivities. 3. Compute UQ three ways: EKI (derivative-free, 100–200 members), SVGD (50–100 particles, adjoint gradients) and low-rank Laplace at the MAP point (randomised eigensolver, r≈200r\approx200–500500). 4. Reference posterior: on a 64364^3 downscaled version, run long HMC (HMCLab) as ground truth. Compare marginal variances, KL or Wasserstein distance per cell, and calibration. 5. Metrics: variance ratio against HMC (target 0.7–1.3), compute cost (forward-equivalents), and how the error grows from 64364^3 to 10610^6 cells.

Watch-list (not top-3 yet): VAE/GAN or diffusion latent priors trained on GemPy/Loop ensembles. These are promising for speed, but the 3-D potential-field evidence is thin and there is a memorisation and prior-misspecification risk.

D. Five open research gaps

  1. Calibrated UQ for 3-property joint inversion at 10610^6 cells. No published work combines petrophysical (GMM) coupling, ≥3 physics, remanence-aware magnetics and a validated posterior at production scale. PGI is deterministic; Ghalehnoei’s (2022) Bayesian gravity–magnetic work is two-physics.
  2. Probabilistic coupling under remanence. The joint posterior over (ρ,𝐌vector,σ)(\rho,\mathbf{M}\ \text{vector},\sigma) is non-Gaussian. Amplitude/MVI parameterisations add non-linearity, and no standard prior on remanence direction exists for joint Bayesian schemes.
  3. Uncertain coupling strength and form. The weights λ\lambda, the GMM component count and the relevance of the cross-gradient term are tuned by hand. Hierarchical Bayes or evidence-based model selection between coupling types (structural, petrophysical, none) is largely unexplored for potential fields. The evidence itself is contested (Mosegaard 2023, arXiv:2308.05858; not read in full).
  4. Bridging geology-parameterised and voxel inversions. Implicit-model posteriors are well calibrated but misspecified when unit topology is wrong. Voxel posteriors are flexible but poorly calibrated. Hybrid “geometry + residual field” models, with consistent likelihoods (Blockworlds aliasing) and gradients through the geomodeller, are an open design space.
  5. Learned priors and SBI for potential fields with distribution-shift guarantees. Gravity and magnetic SBI or diffusion work is nearly absent (Rakoczi & Hammond 2023 is an exception). Methods are needed for physics-corrected amortisation (à la Siahkoohi et al. 2023), memorisation diagnostics, and training-set design for under-mapped terranes such as Indian cratons and the Deccan basalts.

2.2 Critical points to check in any such paper

Over-fitting. Look for:

  • the final misfit compared with the number of data (χ² ≈ N),
  • how β was selected (L-curve, discrepancy principle, GCV),
  • a residual map (it must look like random noise, with no geology in it),
  • validation on independent data (hold-out lines, drill holes).

A paper that gives none of these cannot show that it does not over-fit.

Negative susceptibility explained as remanence. Natural rocks have almost no negative susceptibility: diamagnetic minerals such as quartz and calcite are near −1×10⁻⁵ SI. A negative value in an inversion comes from one of these causes:

  1. Remanent magnetisation at an angle to the present field.
  2. An error in the regional field or the IGRF (wrong epoch, levelling, survey merge).
  3. No positivity bound, combined with smoothness regularisation. The smoothing makes side-lobes around real bodies.
  4. Over-fitting of noise.
  5. Depth weighting and a reference model that move the anomaly.
  6. Self-demagnetisation in very high-susceptibility bodies (above about 0.1 SI).

To claim cause 1, the paper must exclude causes 2–6. The standard tools are MVI, inversion of amplitude data, and measured Koenigsberger ratios (Q) from the GTK petrophysical database.

Memory and compute. Record the exact method (data-space solve, compression, octree mesh, on-the-fly kernels, GPU) and its costs: accuracy loss, time per iteration, and the limits it puts on which regularisation is possible.

2.3 Synthetic stress tests (done)

We built a controlled test in SimPEG 0.26 (Python) on a 4-CPU cloud machine. All code and results are in the repository.

Setup:

  • Mesh: 24 × 24 × 12 cells of 25 m (6,912 cells).
  • Data: total-field magnetic data at 30 m height on a 24 × 24 grid (576 points). The field is B₀ = 52,000 nT, inclination 75°, declination 10°, similar to Finland.
  • Hold-out lines: every 4th flight line (144 data) is used only for testing. The inversion uses the remaining 432 data.
  • Bodies:
    • Body A: χ = 0.05, induced magnetisation only.
    • Body B: χ = 0.03. In the remanence case, body B also has reversed remanence (Q = 3, inclination −60°, declination 190°).
  • Noise: Gaussian, with standard deviation 2 % of the value + 2 nT.
  • Inversion: scalar susceptibility, L2 smallness + smoothness, sensitivity weighting, projected Gauss–Newton.

The common solutions are:

  • Wavelet or adaptive compression of G: 10–100× smaller, with some accuracy loss.
  • Octree meshes: fewer cells at depth.
  • Data-space formulation: solves an N × N system, which helps when N ≪ M.
  • Matrix-free GPU kernels: compute G on the fly instead of storing it.
  • Equivalent sources and tiling: cut the problem into tiles.

The reproduction must record the memory, time and accuracy for each approach against a dense reference on a small tile.

3.2 Ideas from other inverse-problem fields

Notation used throughout

  • 𝐦ρ\mathbf m_\rho: density contrast in each mesh cell.
  • 𝐦κ\mathbf m_\kappa: magnetic susceptibility in each mesh cell.
  • 𝐝g=𝐆g𝐦ρ+𝐞g\mathbf d_g = \mathbf G_g\mathbf m_\rho + \mathbf e_g: gravity data. The forward operator is linear.
  • 𝐝m=𝐆m𝐦κ+𝐞m\mathbf d_m = \mathbf G_m\mathbf m_\kappa + \mathbf e_m: magnetic data. The forward operator is linear if remanence and self-demagnetisation are ignored.
  • The current coupling is assumed to be probabilistic, for example a petrophysical Gaussian mixture or a correlation prior.

Two facts about potential fields drive most of the transfer decisions below: 1. The forward operators are linear, dense and cheap. Methods that need many forward solves, such as HMC, ensembles and diffusion guidance, are affordable here. Surrogates such as FNOs and PINNs bring little gain. 2. The problem has a very large null space and no intrinsic depth resolution. Most of the gain comes from better priors, such as structural, sparse or learned priors. More data-fit machinery helps less.

A1. Medical imaging

A1.1 Compressed sensing and total variation (CT and MRI)

  • Key paper: Lustig, Donoho and Pauly (2007), Sparse MRI, Magn. Reson. Med. 58(6):1182-1195. DOI 10.1002/mrm.21391.
  • What it solves: recovering images from heavily undersampled data by promoting sparsity in a transform domain, using ℓ1, TV or wavelet penalties.
  • How to transfer:
    • Potential-field codes already use ℓp “compact” and “blocky” norms. Heagy et al. used sparse and compact norms for carbon-mineralisation targets (arXiv 2203.13894).
    • The new elements are three things:
      1. Sparsity in a learned or multiscale dictionary, such as 3D wavelets on the octree, instead of in cell values.
      2. Isotropic 3D TV, which favours sharp-edged bodies, instead of separate anisotropic ℓp-on-gradient terms.
      3. Proper convex solvers (ADMM or primal-dual) instead of IRLS.
    • Tomofast-x v2 already uses wavelet compression (Ogarko et al. 2024, GMD 17:2325, DOI 10.5194/gmd-17-2325-2024).
  • Expected gain: sharper boundaries and better volume estimates for compact bodies such as serpentinised or ultramafic lenses. There is no gain in depth resolution unless depth weighting is kept.
  • Risk: low. The solutions are MAP estimates only, and the results depend on the regularisation weights.
  • Effort: 5-10 days.

A1.2 Parallel level sets and structure-guided TV (PET-MRI)

  • Key papers:
    • Ehrhardt et al. (2015), Joint reconstruction of PET-MRI by exploiting structural similarity, Inverse Problems 31:015001. DOI 10.1088/0266-5611/31/1/015001.
    • Ehrhardt et al. (2016), PET reconstruction with an anatomical MRI prior using parallel level sets, IEEE TMI 35(9). DOI 10.1109/TMI.2016.2549601.
    • Ehrhardt and Betcke (2016), Multicontrast MRI reconstruction with structure-guided total variation, SIAM J. Imaging Sci. 9(3):1084. DOI 10.1137/15M1047325.
  • What it solves: coupling two images that share edges, but not intensities, without a petrophysical relationship.
  • How the two coupling terms work:
    • Parallel level sets (PLS): penalises ∑i(∥∇u∥∥∇v∥−|⟨∇u,∇v⟩|)\sum_i \big(\|\nabla u\|\|\nabla v\| - |\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle|\big), or a smoothed version. Unlike the cross-gradient ∥∇u×∇v∥2\|\nabla u\times\nabla v\|^2, it is scale-aware and does not vanish where one gradient is zero.
    • Directional TV (dTV): the projection (I−ξξ⊤)∇u\big(I-\xi\xi^\top\big)\nabla u removes the gradient component along the reference edge direction ξ=∇v/∥∇v∥η\xi=\nabla v/\|\nabla v\|_\eta.
  • How to transfer:
    • Drop-in replacement for the cross-gradient between 𝐦ρ\mathbf m_\rho and 𝐦κ\mathbf m_\kappa, and later for the third property.
    • It extends directly to 3 or more properties through joint TV (A1.2b below) or pairwise PLS.
    • Background on cross-gradient and structural joint inversion in geophysics:
      • Haber and Oldenburg (1997), Inverse Problems 13:63. DOI 10.1088/0266-5611/13/1/006.
      • Gallardo and Meju (2003), GRL 30. DOI 10.1029/2003GL017370.
      • Haber and Holtzman Gazit (2013), Model fusion and joint inversion, Surv. Geophys. 34:675. DOI 10.1007/s10712-013-9232-4. This paper covers joint TV as a convex structural coupling.
  • Expected gain:
    • Joint TV is convex, so it gives better-conditioned and less local-minimum-prone coupling than the cross-gradient, which is non-convex.
    • The coupling weight is easier to tune.
    • Edges are preserved where one property has no contrast.
  • Risk: low to medium. These terms enforce co-located edges, which is wrong where magnetite-destructive alteration decouples density and susceptibility boundaries.
  • Effort: 4-8 days. Implement it as a SimPEG regularisation object with gradient and Hessian approximation.

A1.3 Learned unrolled reconstruction (learned primal-dual)

  • Key paper: Adler and Öktem (2018), Learned primal-dual reconstruction, IEEE TMI 37(6):1322. DOI 10.1109/TMI.2018.2799231; arXiv 1707.06474.
  • What it solves: fast, high-quality reconstruction by unrolling an iterative solver. CNNs replace the proximal operators, and the forward operator stays inside the network.
  • How to transfer:
    • Unroll about 10 iterations of the primal-dual algorithm using 𝐆g,𝐆m\mathbf G_g,\mathbf G_m and their adjoints.
    • Train on synthetic geology, for example the Noddyverse dataset used by arXiv 2603.06829.
    • The dense 𝐆\mathbf G for 1e5 cells is heavy but can be compressed or computed matrix-free.
  • Expected gain:
    • Fast amortised inversion: seconds per survey.
    • The output is a strong initial model or proposal for a sampler.
  • Risk: high.
    • The result is only as good as the training geology, which needs Indian targets such as Deccan basalt and ultramafics.
    • The network is mesh-specific and gives no calibrated uncertainty.
  • Effort: 20-30 days.

A1.4 Electrical impedance tomography (EIT)

  • Key paper: Hamilton and Hauptmann (2018), Deep D-bar: real-time EIT imaging with deep neural networks, IEEE TMI 37(10). arXiv 1711.03180. The DOI was not shown in the results.
  • What it solves: EIT is mathematically the same as DC resistivity. The paper post-processes a fast, blurry direct reconstruction with a U-Net to sharpen it.
  • How to transfer:
    • Mostly relevant to Task 4: DC/IP as the third property.
    • The idea of a “cheap blurry reconstruction plus learned deblurring” also applies to potential fields. Feed a smooth Tikhonov or L2 inversion into a U-Net trained on synthetic pairs.
  • Expected gain: cheap sharpening, and a good starting model.
  • Risk: it hallucinates structure and gives no uncertainty.
  • Effort: 8-12 days.

A2. Astrophysics

A2.1 CLEAN (radio interferometry)

  • Key paper: Högbom (1974), A&AS 15:417. This is a classic paper with no DOI; it was cited in the results via Cornwell (2009), A&A, DOI 10.1051/0004-6361/200912148.
  • What it solves: greedy deconvolution. The method repeatedly finds the brightest residual peak and subtracts a scaled point-spread function.
  • How to transfer:
    • A greedy “source-body CLEAN” for potential fields.
    • Iteratively place the cell or prism with the largest normalised correlation to the residual, after depth weighting, and subtract its response. This is matching pursuit with 𝐆\mathbf G columns.
    • The result is a fast sparse initial model and an estimate of the number of bodies.
  • Expected gain: a fast, interpretable compact-body starting model, useful as an initialiser for the sampler.
  • Risk: depth ambiguity makes greedy selection biased toward shallow cells unless depth-weighted.
  • Effort: 3-5 days.

A2.2 Sparsity Averaging Reweighted Analysis (SARA)

  • Key paper: Carrillo, McEwen and Wiaux (2012), MNRAS 426:1223. DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2966.2012.21605.x; arXiv 1205.3123.
  • What it solves: a reweighted ℓ1 penalty in a concatenation of several wavelet bases (average sparsity), solved by convex splitting.
  • How to transfer:
    • Replace single-basis ℓp regularisation with reweighted analysis sparsity over a concatenation of 3D Daubechies wavelets plus a gradient (TV) basis.
    • Works on a tensor mesh, or on octree leaves via a wavelet-on-tree.
  • Expected gain: better multi-scale structure than a pure TV or ℓ0-like penalty: broad basins plus compact bodies together.
  • Risk: medium. There is solver complexity and the result is still a MAP point estimate.
  • Effort: 6-10 days. Can be combined with A1.1.

A2.3 Event Horizon Telescope imaging and Bayesian imaging with normalising flows

  • Key papers:
    • EHT Collaboration (2019), Paper IV, ApJL 875:L4. DOI 10.3847/2041-8213/ab0e85; arXiv 1906.11241.
    • Sun and Bouman, Deep Probabilistic Imaging (DPI), arXiv 2010.14462 (AAAI 2021).
    • α-DPI, arXiv 2201.08506.
  • What it solves:
    • Paper IV calibrates imaging choices by running many independent pipelines on synthetic data and surveying the parameter space.
    • DPI trains a normalising flow to approximate the posterior by variational inference.
  • How to transfer:
    1. EHT-style “imaging-parameter survey”. Run the joint inversion over a grid of regularisation and coupling hyperparameters on synthetic look-alikes, and keep only settings that pass on all of them. This is a cheap and honest way to estimate uncertainty due to user choices.
    2. DPI-style variational posterior. Train a flow qϕ(𝐦)q_\phi(\mathbf m) to minimise KL(q∥p(𝐦∣𝐝))\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p(\mathbf m\mid\mathbf d)). The geophysics analogue is Zhao, Curtis and Zhang (2022), Bayesian seismic tomography using normalizing flows, GJI 228:213, DOI 10.1093/gji/ggab298.
  • Expected gain: posterior samples for a 1e4-1e5-cell model at much lower cost than MCMC.
  • Risk:
    • The flow can miss modes; α-DPI notes trouble with disconnected modes.
    • Flows over a full 3D mesh are memory-heavy, so use a low-dimensional parameterisation.
  • Effort: survey 3-5 days; DPI 15-25 days.

A2.4 Gravitational-lensing mass reconstruction

  • Key papers:
    • Kaiser and Squires (1993), ApJ 404:441. DOI 10.1086/172297.
    • Remy et al., Probabilistic mass mapping with neural score estimation, arXiv 2201.05561 (A&A 2023). This paper combines a Gaussian (Wiener) analytic prior with a learned score correction, and samples with annealed HMC. Code: jax-lensing.
  • What it solves: recovering a projected mass density from shear data. It is very close to potential-field inversion, being linear, Fourier-diagonal and noisy.
  • How to transfer:
    • The Remy recipe transfers almost directly to 2D or 2.5D gravity.
    • The prior score is (Gaussian-process score) plus (learned residual score), sampled by annealed HMC.
    • For 3D, apply it in a reduced space: depth slabs or a latent space.
  • Expected gain: principled posterior samples with a physically motivated Gaussian backbone. The learned part only corrects non-Gaussian features such as sharp bodies.
  • Risk: medium. Training data are needed for the residual score.
  • Effort: 12-20 days.

A2.5 Hamiltonian Monte Carlo at scale

  • Key paper: Fichtner, Zunino and Gebraad (2019), HMC solution of tomographic inverse problems, GJI 216:1344. DOI 10.1093/gji/ggy496. The abstract reports thousands of unknowns on a laptop, with tuned mass matrices.
  • What it solves: gradient-based MCMC that scales to high dimensions.
  • How to transfer:
    • Potential fields are an ideal case. Gradients 𝐆⊤𝐂d−1(𝐆𝐦−𝐝)\mathbf G^\top\mathbf C_d^{-1}(\mathbf G\mathbf m-\mathbf d) are a single matrix-vector product.
    • A good mass matrix is 𝐌≈𝐆⊤𝐂d−1𝐆+𝐂m−1\mathbf M\approx \mathbf G^\top\mathbf C_d^{-1}\mathbf G + \mathbf C_m^{-1} (Gauss-Newton Hessian), or a diagonal or low-rank version of it.
    • Use HMC for the continuous properties inside a Gibbs loop, with discrete lithology labels updated separately (see Part B (a)).
    • Use bounded or transformed variables, such as log-susceptibility.
  • Expected gain: true posterior samples for 1e4-1e5 cells, including a non-Gaussian mixture prior. These enable volume-at-depth probability estimates of reactive rock, which matter directly for carbon-mineralisation decisions.
  • Risk: medium.
    • Mixture priors create multimodality.
    • Mass matrix and step tuning are needed.
  • Effort: 8-15 days with JAX or PyTorch autodiff.

A3. Machine learning

A3.1 Diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) and DDRM

  • Key papers:
    • Chung et al. (2023), Diffusion Posterior Sampling for General Noisy Inverse Problems, ICLR. arXiv 2209.14687.
    • Kawar, Elad, Ermon and Song (2022), DDRM, NeurIPS. arXiv 2201.11793.
  • What they solve: using a pretrained diffusion prior to sample p(𝐦∣𝐝)p(\mathbf m\mid\mathbf d) for noisy linear or nonlinear forward models, without retraining for each problem.
    • DPS adds the likelihood gradient evaluated at the denoised estimate 𝐦̂0(𝐦t)\hat{\mathbf m}_0(\mathbf m_t).
    • DDRM works in the SVD basis of a linear operator.
  • How to transfer:
    • The gravity and magnetic operators are linear, so both methods apply.
    • DDRM needs the SVD of 𝐆\mathbf G, which is feasible after compression or with a randomised SVD.
    • DPS needs only 𝐆\mathbf G and 𝐆⊤\mathbf G^\top.
    • To run on a 3D mesh, train the diffusion model in a VAE latent space (latent diffusion), as done for gravity and magnetics below.
  • Expected gain:
    • Geologically realistic samples with sharp contacts.
    • The posterior spread reflects the prior’s notion of plausible geology.
  • Risk: high.
    • DPS is known to be miscalibrated. Its likelihood approximation is crude and it under- or over-estimates the spread.
    • Results are dominated by how representative the training geology is.
    • Mitigation: run a few MCMC correction steps (Metropolis-adjusted) after DPS, or check calibration on synthetics.
  • Effort: 25-40 days, including generating a training set.

A3.2 Existing applications to gravity and magnetic inversion (2022-2026)

  1. Joint 3D Gravity and Magnetic Inversion via Rectified Flow and Ginzburg-Landau Guidance. arXiv 2603.06829 (March 2026).
    • Trains a 3D VAE on Noddyverse density and susceptibility volumes.
    • Runs latent rectified-flow posterior sampling, with a Ginzburg-Landau (phase-field) regulariser that pushes toward ore-like binary bodies.
    • This is the closest existing work to the Alt Carbon problem, and it is the paper to reproduce first.
  2. Three-dimensional inversion of gravity data using implicit neural representations. Mishra et al., arXiv 2510.17876. A v2 in 2026 adds “and scientific machine learning” to the title.
    • Density is represented as a coordinate MLP with positional encoding, fitted directly through the forward operator. No mesh is predefined.
  3. Depth-Aware Implicit Neural Representation Priors for 3D Gravity Inversion. arXiv 2608.08959 (2026).
    • Uses per-depth-slab INRs with separate Fourier-feature bandwidths, plus TV, sparsity and inter-slab consistency penalties.
  4. Regularized ensemble Kalman inversion for gravity data, mineral and ore deposits. Sci. Rep. (2025). DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-30141-y.
  5. Diffusion posterior sampling for multivariate subsurface property generation. arXiv 2507.15809 (2025).
    • Adapts DPS (Chung et al.) for probabilistic inversion of several properties together.
    • UNVERIFIED: the exact title and whether it targets potential fields; the results only described it as multivariate subsurface generation.
  6. GMNet, CNN joint gravity-magnetic inversion. Remote Sensing (2024). UNVERIFIED (no DOI shown).
  7. Enhanced 3D Gravity Inversion Using ResU-Net with Density Logging Constraints. arXiv 2601.02890.
  8. Diffusion-based posterior sampling in full waveform inversion. arXiv 2512.12797. This is seismic, not potential-field, but it is relevant to a seismic third property.

A3.3 Neural fields and implicit neural representations (INRs)

  • Key paper: arXiv 2510.17876; extension arXiv 2608.08959. See A3.2.
  • What it solves: a mesh-free, continuous model parameterisation with implicit smoothness controlled by Fourier-feature bandwidth.
  • How to transfer:
    • Use one MLP with several outputs: fθ(x,y,z)→(ρ,κ,η)f_\theta(x,y,z)\to(\rho,\kappa,\eta).
    • The shared hidden layers give implicit structural coupling.
    • Each physics queries the field at its own mesh’s cell centres or quadrature points. This removes the octree-versus-PDE-mesh mismatch (see Part B).
    • For uncertainty, use deep ensembles, or Bayesian last-layer or SWAG.
  • Expected gain:
    • Natural multi-physics coupling.
    • Resolution independence.
    • Compact parameters, about 1e5 weights, which are independent of the size of the domain.
  • Risk:
    • Spectral bias toward smooth fields, and the results are sensitive to the bandwidth hyperparameter.
    • Uncertainty from ensembles is not a true posterior.
    • Optimisation is non-convex.
  • Effort: 8-12 days for gravity and magnetics; plus 5 days for a third property.

A3.4 Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)

  • Key paper: Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis (2019), J. Comput. Phys. 378:686. DOI 10.1016/j.jcp.2018.10.045. A gravity PINN that embeds the Poisson equation also exists in the CEUR-WS Vol-4224 short paper (Nurtas and Kenzhebayeva); it has no DOI and is UNVERIFIED as to venue details.
  • Transfer: low value for gravity and magnetics. The integral forward model is exact and cheap, so enforcing ∇2ϕ=4πGρ\nabla^2\phi=4\pi G\rho softly is worse.
  • Where it helps: only for a PDE-based third physics (DC/EM) where meshing is painful, or for continuation of data to irregular surfaces.
  • Risk: high, because PINN inverse problems are hard to train.
  • Effort: 15+ days. Not recommended.

A3.5 Fourier neural operators (FNOs) as surrogates

  • Key paper: Li et al. (2021), Fourier Neural Operator for Parametric PDEs, ICLR. arXiv 2010.08895.
  • Geophysics examples:
    • EFKAN, MT forward modelling, arXiv 2502.02195.
    • Extended FNO for MT, IEEE TGRS 2022. UNVERIFIED DOI.
  • Transfer:
    • No need for gravity and magnetics, whose forward models are already cheap.
    • Very useful for the third property. A DC/IP, EM or MT forward solve on a 3D mesh costs seconds to minutes, and an FNO surrogate makes HMC, EKI or DPS loops affordable.
    • Use the surrogate only inside the sampler, with periodic true-solver corrections. Delayed-acceptance MCMC uses the surrogate for screening and the true solver for acceptance.
  • Risk: surrogate error biases the posterior.
  • Effort: 15-25 days, including training data.

A3.6 Simulation-based inference (SBI)

  • Key paper: Cranmer, Brehmer and Louppe (2020), PNAS 117(48):30055. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1912789117; arXiv 1911.01429.
  • What it solves: amortised posteriors via neural posterior or likelihood estimation when the likelihood is intractable but simulation is easy.
  • Transfer: best for low-dimensional parametric questions, not voxel models. Examples:
    • Depth to top, thickness, dip and volume of an ultramafic body.
    • Fraction of reactive rock above 300 m.
    • Simulate from a geological prior and train a neural posterior estimator on (summary statistics of gravity and magnetic data) → (quantities of interest).
  • Expected gain:
    • Direct, calibrated (checkable by simulation-based calibration) posteriors on the decision variables that Alt Carbon cares about.
    • Instant inference for new surveys.
  • Risk: prior misspecification, and summary-statistic design.
  • Effort: 10-15 days.

A4. Data assimilation: ensemble Kalman methods

  • Key papers:
    • Evensen (1994), JGR 99(C5):10143. DOI 10.1029/94JC00572. UNVERIFIED: the DOI came from EarthRef only.
    • Iglesias, Law and Stuart (2013), Ensemble Kalman methods for inverse problems, Inverse Problems 29:045001. DOI 10.1088/0266-5611/29/4/045001; arXiv 1209.2736.
    • Gravity application: Sci. Rep. 2025. DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-30141-y.
  • What it solves: derivative-free ensemble inversion with approximate uncertainty, using only black-box forward runs.
  • How to transfer:
    • The ensemble is drawn from the current probabilistic prior, for example GMM samples conditioned on geology.
    • Iterate EKI with localisation and inflation.
    • The key advantage is for Task 4. A DC/IP or MT forward code can be used without adjoints, and all three physics are updated together through the ensemble cross-covariance. That covariance automatically carries the coupling present in the prior ensemble.
  • Expected gain: fast multi-physics inversion with approximate uncertainty, and very easy parallelisation.
  • Risk:
    • The results are Gaussian-ish, and the ensemble collapses.
    • Uncertainty is underestimated with small ensembles; localisation is needed with fewer than about 200 members.
    • Non-Gaussian lithology mixtures are handled poorly. Mitigation: apply EKI in a latent space, or use a Gaussian-anamorphosis transform.
  • Effort: 6-10 days.

Choosing the third property. This choice is for Alt Carbon’s targets: reactive basalts and ultramafics, and serpentinisation or carbonation fronts. - DC resistivity / IP chargeability is the most natural choice. - Serpentinisation produces magnetite, which raises susceptibility and chargeability and lowers density. B3. Three candidate formulations

(a) Latent lithology field with a petrophysical Gaussian mixture (hierarchical Bayesian, Potts/MRF)

The update is 𝐦∣⋅∼𝒩(𝐦̂,𝐇−1)\mathbf m\mid\cdot\sim\mathcal N(\hat{\mathbf m},\mathbf H^{-1}) with 𝐇=∑j𝐏j⊤𝐆j⊤𝐂j−1𝐆j𝐏j+blkdiag(𝚺zi−1)\mathbf H=\sum_j\mathbf P_j^\top\mathbf G_j^\top\mathbf C_j^{-1}\mathbf G_j\mathbf P_j+\mathrm{blkdiag}(\boldsymbol\Sigma_{z_i}^{-1}). Link to PGI: if β=0\beta=0 and 𝐳\mathbf z is marginalised, the prior reduces to the GMM prior of Astic et al. (2021). The MAP of this formulation is PGI with a spatial lithology term, so the existing SimPEG PGI code is a validated MAP baseline.

Pros: - Interpretable; it outputs P(zi=reactive rock∣𝐝)P(z_i=\text{reactive rock}\mid\mathbf d) directly. - Handles partially decoupled properties. - Hierarchical noise means no hand-tuned data weights.

Cons: - Multimodality: label switching, and 𝐳\mathbf z mixing at large β\beta. - Needs petrophysical data for KK units.

(b) Structural Gibbs prior: cross-gradient or joint total variation

p(𝐦,𝛕∣𝐝,𝛂,λ)∝∏j𝒩(𝐝j∣ℱj(𝐏j𝐦),𝐂j(𝛕j))exp(−∑p=1Pαp∥𝐖p(𝐦(p)−𝐦ref(p))∥22−λΦs(𝐦))p(𝛕) \boxed{ p(\mathbf m,\boldsymbol\tau\mid\mathbf d,\boldsymbol\alpha,\lambda)\ \propto\ \prod_{j}\mathcal N\!\big(\mathbf d_j\mid\mathcal F_j(\mathbf P_j\mathbf m),\mathbf C_j(\boldsymbol\tau_j)\big) \ \exp\!\Big(-\sum_{p=1}^{P}\alpha_p\,\big\|\mathbf W_p(\mathbf m^{(p)}-\mathbf m^{(p)}_{\mathrm{ref}})\big\|_2^2\ -\ \lambda\,\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathbf m)\Big)\ p(\boldsymbol\tau) }

The structural term Φs\Phi_{\mathrm{s}} can take one of three forms:

  • Cross-gradient, summed over pairs: Φxg(𝐦)=∑p<q∑iVi∥∇h𝐦̃i(p)×∇h𝐦̃i(q)∥22,𝐦̃(p)=𝐦(p)/sp \Phi_{\mathrm{xg}}(\mathbf m)=\sum_{p<q}\sum_{i}V_i\,\big\|\nabla_h\tilde{\mathbf m}^{(p)}_i\times\nabla_h\tilde{\mathbf m}^{(q)}_i\big\|_2^2 ,\qquad \tilde{\mathbf m}^{(p)}=\mathbf m^{(p)}/s_p

Symbols: - ∇h\nabla_h is the octree cell-gradient operator (face-to-cell averaged). - ViV_i is the cell volume. - sps_p is a property scale that makes properties comparable, for example a robust standard deviation. - αp\alpha_p are smallness and smoothness weights. - λ\lambda is the coupling strength.

The posterior is a Gibbs or MRF density. Its normaliser Z(𝛂,λ)Z(\boldsymbol\alpha,\lambda) is intractable. Two consequences follow: 1. Treat (𝛂,λ)(\boldsymbol\alpha,\lambda) as fixed. Choose them with the discrepancy principle (each χj2≈Nj\chi^2_j\approx N_j), cross-validation, or an EHT-style survey (A2.3). 2. To sample, use MALA or HMC on the smoothed (ε>0\varepsilon>0) potential. JTV is log-concave when the forward model is linear, so samplers mix well for gravity + magnetics. The cross-gradient term is non-convex, so expect poor mixing there.

Pros: - No petrophysical relationships needed. This is right when chargeability-density links are unknown (Moorkamp et al. 2011 conclusion). - Easy to add to existing code.

Cons: - Assumes co-located boundaries. - Gives no lithology probabilities directly, so it needs post-hoc clustering. - Uncertainty depends on λ\lambda, which is not inferred.

(c) Learned joint prior: normalising flow or diffusion

Let 𝒢ϕ\mathcal G_\phi be a generative model trained on multi-property 3D realisations. To build the training set: - Sample structural geology from a geological simulator, such as Noddy/Noddyverse (as in arXiv 2603.06829) or GemPy with Indian-analogue stratigraphy. - Sample (Δρ,κ,logσ,η)(\Delta\rho,\kappa,\log\sigma,\eta) per unit from the petrophysical GMM of (a). - Add alteration fields, for example serpentinisation degree.

B4. Mesh mismatch: octree potential-field mesh vs PDE meshes

The problem: - Gravity and magnetics are computed by integral equations on any cell set. A topography-conforming octree with no padding is ideal. - DC/IP, EM and MT need a PDE mesh with large padding (5-10× survey size), boundary conditions, fine cells near electrodes, and often different refinement. - Seismic traveltimes need a regular or tetrahedral eikonal grid.

Recommended design: 1. One model mesh ℳ\mathcal M. Use a shared octree refined under all surveys, at the finest resolution any physics can resolve. All unknowns and all priors (GMM, Potts, JTV) live on ℳ\mathcal M. 2. Per-physics forward meshes ℳj\mathcal M_j, with linear maps 𝐏j:ℳ→ℳj\mathbf P_j:\mathcal M\to\mathcal M_j. - Use conservative volume-weighted averaging for a fine-to-coarse map; for log-conductivity, do this in log space. - Use injection for the coarse-to-fine map. - Padding cells in ℳj\mathcal M_j are filled from a fixed background value or a single extra “halfspace” parameter. - Gradients pass through 𝐏j⊤\mathbf P_j^\top. - In SimPEG this is a chain of maps: InjectActiveCells ∘ Mesh2Mesh ∘ ExpMap ∘ Projection, with one wire per property. 3. Simplest route for DC/IP: run DC/IP forward on the same octree, extended with padding cells. SimPEG solves DC/IP with finite volumes on a TreeMesh. Then 𝐏e\mathbf P_e is just active-cell injection, and the mismatch largely disappears. 4. Mesh-free alternative: a neural field fθ(𝐱)f_\theta(\mathbf x) (A3.3) is queried at each ℳj\mathcal M_j’s cell centres. Use quadrature, averaging several points per coarse cell, to avoid aliasing. 5. Pitfalls: - Resolution mismatch biases structural coupling. Gradients of a property defined on a coarse forward mesh are piecewise-constant on fine model cells, so compute ∇h\nabla_h on ℳ\mathcal M only. - Topography must be identical across meshes. - Depth weighting 𝐖\mathbf W applies to potential-field properties only.

B5. Data weighting between datasets

Datasets differ in N (10³-10⁵ stations for airborne magnetics vs about 10³ for DC/IP), noise characterisation, and correlated errors. If nothing is done, the largest or least-noisy dataset dominates.

Options, from most principled to least: 1. Hierarchical noise (default for (a) and (c)). B6. Recommended first prototype

Formulation (a), with DC resistivity + IP chargeability as the third property, built in SimPEG on one octree.

  1. Week 1-2: synthetic benchmark.
    • Build a model of a serpentinised ultramafic or basalt body with a carbonated cap.
    • Use 4 units: host, fresh ultramafic, serpentinite and carbonated zone.
    • Properties: (Δρ,logκ,logσ,η)(\Delta\rho,\log\kappa,\log\sigma,\eta).
    • Simulate gravity, magnetics, and pole-dipole DC/IP lines.
  2. Week 2-3: MAP baseline.
    • Joint PGI with 3 physics, following Astic et al. (2021).
    • SimPEG’s PGI handles multiple physics through combined DataMisfit and PGI regularisation.
    • Compare against (b) with JTV or cross-gradient as the structural baseline.
  3. Week 3-5: sampled posterior.
    • Blocked Gibbs for (a): Potts 𝐳\mathbf z updates; Gaussian 𝐦\mathbf m update using linearised IP and DC fixed at the MAP conductivity. This keeps the conditional Gaussian.
    • Then switch to HMC on logσ\log\sigma with adjoint gradients, or EKI (A4) if adjoints are a bottleneck.
    • Hierarchical noise scales for the data weighting.
  4. Outputs:
    • P(zi=serpentinite∣𝐝)P(z_i=\text{serpentinite}\mid\mathbf d).
    • Posterior volume of reactive rock above depth z*z^*.
    • The improvement over gravity + magnetics alone, as reduced posterior variance near surface.
  5. Later: replace the Potts/GMM prior with a learned latent prior (c1) trained on the same synthetic family, and compare calibration.

Why (a) first: - It extends the current probabilistic coupling naturally. - Its MAP has an existing validated implementation (PGI). - It outputs lithology probabilities directly, which are the quantity Alt Carbon needs. - Each Gibbs block is simple. - (b) serves as a cheap baseline and (c) as the research extension.
